## Summary

Between ad reads for Edward Jones, Home Depot/Frigidaire (up to $500 off select appliances, free delivery over $998, 15+ cooking modes, pizza in two minutes, October 21 US-only offer), Jonah Goldberg’s Remnant solo segment discusses right-of-center podcast economics, Trump’s AI/science claims, and genealogy. He says a Steve-sent list of the top 50 right-wing/conservative podcasts shows YouTube metrics and algorithmic exposure increasingly matter, with younger cord-cutters treating podcasts as TV, and he contrasts Remnant, Dispatch, and Advisory Opinions with Sean Hannity and Candace Owens while urging subscriptions/reviews. He mocks Trump’s repeated claim that his MIT physicist uncle gives him special understanding of AI and nuclear issues, calls it a transitive-property/Lamarckian shortcut, notes Trump’s Truth Social assertion that using “artificial intelligence” instead of “superintelligence” makes one an enemy, and links it to aging, cognitive compression, and Twitter-style shorthand. Goldberg also says ChatGPT is useful for quick genealogy, recounts his Harvard physicist grandfather’s possible proximity/circle to Robert Oppenheimer and Navy sonar patents, and teases death penalty, socialism, and Yoram Hazony.

Actionable takeaways: treat podcast rankings as partly YouTube-algorithm artifacts and, if choosing or producing media, track YouTube views/subscriptions and test video-first distribution; for Trump’s claims, separate family association from expertise and ask what evidence supports heritability of scientific ability, what the actual AI terminology policy is, and what technical sources back AI/nuclear statements. For personal research, use AI-assisted genealogy with precise prompts and follow-up questions, then verify against primary records; if family history includes patents, Navy work, or Oppenheimer-era physics, search patent databases, university archives, and institutional records. The health/cognition angle is to watch for age-related reliance on shorthand and metaphor in public figures and oneself, and to distinguish compressed narratives from substantiated arguments.
