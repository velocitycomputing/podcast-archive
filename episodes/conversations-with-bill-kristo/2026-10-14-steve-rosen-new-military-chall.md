## Summary

Steve Rosen, a retired Harvard international-relations professor now working with the American Academy of Strategic Education, told Bill Kristol that the U.S. homeland is no longer a secure rear area against non-nuclear precision attacks and that incremental adaptation is failing. He cited his 1986 article on low-observable technology, precision strike, and advanced data processing; Ukraine’s 2024 deep strikes on Russian bomber bases, submarine bases, and missile factories; attacks on U.S. facilities in Bahrain and a Middle East naval logistics hub; urgent evacuation of B-1 bombers from British bases after infiltrated precision-strike threats; British intelligence warnings about Iranian targeting of U.S. bases in Europe; a December 2023 drone incursion over Langley Air Force Base; and a March 2026 drone incursion over Barksdale Air Force Base. He noted a NORAD deputy commander’s statement that the U.S. lacks effective drone defenses in the continental United States, Secretary of Defense Hexath’s new homeland-defense advisory council, Air Force resistance to aircraft shelters, and the need for cheaper underground hardening. Rosen also argued that defense alone loses, aircraft carriers cannot operate near Iran because of Houthi anti-access threats, and the U.S. needs non-nuclear coercive/deterrent capabilities against China, Russia, and Iran. He broadened the threat beyond drones to cyber, counterintelligence, and personnel targeting: OPM file compromises, AI-generated voice impersonation of a North Dakota ICBM crew member, encroaching development near Whiteman and Schriever bases, smuggling of precision weapons, Chinese and Iranian influence operations, and interagency silos among DoD, FBI, DHS, and intelligence agencies. He compared today’s mobilization problem to the 1930s-1950s, invoking FDR’s radio addresses, Robert Lovett’s aircraft-production push, Alfred Loomis’s role in the Manhattan Project, Vannevar Bush’s research mobilization, Herman Kahn’s critique of Vietnam bombing logic, and the difficulty of coercing Putin’s Russia or Iran.

For the user, the actionable material is mostly security-policy and research follow-through rather than personal health: no direct health implications are stated, but the episode raises public-safety and civil-liberties tradeoffs around drone defense, base security, personnel protection, and surveillance. Useful decisions include tracking whether DoD, DHS, FBI, and intelligence agencies create a joint counter-drone/counterintelligence task force with clear authority; whether Congress funds base hardening, underground shelters, low-cost drone defenses, and personnel-security upgrades; whether agencies fix OPSEC gaps such as hacked personal phones, AI voice spoofing, and public exposure of cleared personnel; and whether defense firms and critical infrastructure operators treat personnel, supply chains, shipping containers, and encroaching civilian development as attack vectors. Research leads include Rosen’s 1986 article and Foreign Affairs piece, FDR’s prewar radio addresses, the 1938 B-17 procurement reversal, Lovett/Loomis/Bush mobilization cases, NORAD and SOCOM statements, Ukraine/Israel deep-strike examples, and Herman Kahn’s coercion critique. Follow-up questions should ask: What specific systems can defend U.S. bases from drone swarms without harming nearby hospitals or civilians? How will the U.S. build non-nuclear coercive options against China, Russia, and Iran? What legal/ethical limits apply to domestic counterintelligence and personnel monitoring? What metrics—interception rates, shelter construction, incident reporting, cyber remediation, supply-chain screening—would show the U.S. is actually getting out of the “heating water”?
