## Summary

Phillips O'Brien tells Bill Kristol (recorded around September 29, 2026) that the Ukraine war, now past four and a half years, has split into two very different contests. In the air war Russia is winning: the US has cut off Patriot missiles, having used more than half its stock fighting Iran. China has helped Russia build jet-powered drones. Russia can now send thousands of systems a week against Ukrainian cities and steel and military production, and he expects an attempt to cut power and heat this winter. On the ground Ukraine is doing better than expected. It is retaking territory around Lyman and elsewhere in the Donbas with small numbers of skilled soldiers backed by drone cover and by ground robots. In one operation, drones dropped unmanned ground vehicles about 10 km behind Russian lines. Months of mid-range strikes on Russian logistics set this up. Meanwhile 250 Russian soldiers surrendered, and O'Brien says Russia is losing more troops each month than it recruits. He describes European support as a mixed bag. The Nordics, the Baltics and (within limits) Germany are strong, and the Nordics fund Ukrainian companies to build their own weapons. Spain and France give little, and France is slow to field its SAMP/T air defense. O'Brien argues the lesson is a cost war, in which cheap mass beats expensive crewed systems such as aircraft, tanks and large-crew warships. He says the US industrial base and its alliances have both eroded, which would leave the US like Germany in WWII against a China that could out-produce it, as he argues in his book *War in Power*. He thinks the Pentagon under current leadership can't do joined-up strategic thinking. The Iran war's depleted stockpiles are, in his view, a chance to rebuild rather than restore the old system, and he and Kristol agree the US military has been given too much of a pass. The transcript is truncated, so the rest of the episode is not covered.

For you, the practical items are mostly things to watch and read. In the next few months, check three things. First, whether the Lyman-area gains hold or grow, and whether Russia mobilizes Moscow and St. Petersburg, which O'Brien treats as a test of Putin's system. Second, how bad Ukraine's winter air-defense gap gets, and whether the Ukrainian drone defense and the French SAMP/T arrive. Third, whether Ukraine retaliates in kind against Russian power and heat, which O'Brien sees as possible. To go deeper, read O'Brien's Substack and *War in Power*. Its cost-versus-quality and industrial-capacity argument is the main framework he offers for judging US military spending and readiness, and for judging claims that drones or alliances change the balance. If you follow defense policy or invest, apply his stance that more Pentagon money is wasted until leadership and strategy change. He is a critic, and the claims come from one interview, so treat them as his view rather than settled fact. Cross-check the battlefield claims, such as the Lyman advances, the 250 surrenders and the Russian recruitment-versus-loss figures, against independent reporting before acting on them.
