## Summary

Axelrod and Kristol discuss the 2026 midterms as a possible Democratic wave driven by Donald Trump’s low approval ratings—“somewhere in the 30s”—and voter anger over the economy, tariffs, and a war in Iran that Axelrod says has raised gas, diesel, and fertilizer costs and hurt farm states. Key claims include that Democrats need about nine or ten House seats to take the chamber, versus a historical midterm average of roughly 23 to 25, while the Senate map is tougher but has expanded to about eleven competitive states, including Kansas, Nebraska, Mississippi, Iowa, and Texas. Axelrod cites Iowa as a bellwether: Trump won it by 13 points but is now about 10 points underwater, with Democrats potentially picking up two or three congressional seats, winning the governor’s race, and making the Senate race competitive. Named figures and entities include Barack Obama, Tony Fabrizio, Chris Lasvita, James Carville, Mike Johnson, Ashley Henson, Tallerico, Paxton, John Cornyn, El Say, and Abbott. The discussion also covers Republican strategy—outspending Democrats, attacking “radical left” candidates, and betting on backloaded tax cuts—alongside Axelrod’s view that the bet has failed because Trump’s tariffs and Iran war have dominated the political environment. Axelrod warns of election-integrity chaos, including Trump’s continued attacks on mail-in ballots, possible red-state governor interventions in close Senate races, and Mike Johnson’s reluctance to challenge Trump.

For the user, the actionable takeaway is political monitoring rather than health guidance: no direct health implications appear in the supplied material. Concrete research leads include tracking Trump approval on the economy, early-voting turnout, state-level polling in Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, Mississippi, and Texas, and whether Democrats reach the nine-to-ten-seat House threshold or win the Senate by more than a narrow 51-49 margin. Decisions to consider include prioritizing races where tariffs, diesel prices, gas prices, and the Iran war are salient, especially in farm states and border or trade-exposed states. Follow-up questions worth tracking are: whether Republican spending and “radical left” messaging can offset Trump’s unpopularity; whether Texas becomes a resource drain for Republicans because of Tallerico’s fundraising advantage; whether late primary or general-election breaks by vulnerable Republicans matter; and whether election-night disputes in red states could affect close Senate races more than House races.
