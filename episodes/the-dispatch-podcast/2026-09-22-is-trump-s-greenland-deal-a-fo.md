## Summary

The panel (Mike Warren, Kevin Williamson, and retired special forces officer Mike Nelson) discussed Trump's announced deal with Denmark and Greenland. It gives the US "permanent control" over security in Greenland at no cost, and it is expected to be signed soon. Williamson argued that the superlative-heavy announcement signals there is little in it. The 1951 agreement already let the US build bases and station troops there. He also noted that no one can read the text before signing, and that it may be mostly a "plan to have a plan." Nelson said the strategic interest is real: ballistic missile defense, North Atlantic and Arctic naval access, and guarantees that Russia and China are shut out. He argued the US could have had all of that by asking, or by paying a little. The 18-month pressure campaign had real costs, he said. Denmark sent special operations forces to Greenland to prepare for a possible US invasion. Allies are now less willing to join US operations, including the Iran war. The deal also says nothing on Greenland's minerals. And "forever" guarantees are unreliable, since NAFTA and USMCA were both called enduring. They tied this to NATO jitters about a possible Russian test of Article 5 through drones or missiles. They also tied it to Trump's weak domestic position: the Iran war is going badly, and Williamson cited diesel at $6.51 a gallon and Saudi complaints about the Strait of Hormuz. Williamson predicted Trump will move into "blame deflection mode" when bad economic news arrives. The transcript cuts off partway through, before the later segments on Washington Post reporting on unacknowledged US troop deaths in Iran and the DC "ark" plan.

Little here calls for direct action. The main step is to wait for the actual text of the Greenland agreement, expected to be signed around the UN General Assembly this week. When it appears, check whether it has substantive new provisions (a "part B"), whether it covers a Greenlandic independence scenario, and whether it excludes Russia and China as described. Treat the White House's "no cost" and "permanent" claims skeptically until then, since the panel expects real construction costs and a reversible arrangement. Watch for signs of a Russian probe of NATO, such as drone incursions or airspace violations, and for the Pentagon policy review that may reduce US commitments in Europe. If you hold energy-sensitive or diesel-dependent exposure, the panel's economic warning is worth tracking: they expect higher transport and goods costs and a possible market correction. Because the transcript is truncated, the Iran casualty-transparency and DC segments are not covered here.
