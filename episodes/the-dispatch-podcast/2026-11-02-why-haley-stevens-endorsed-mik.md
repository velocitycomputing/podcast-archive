## Summary

Democratic Rep. Haley Stevens, a four-term Michigan House member, endorsed Republican Mike Rogers for Senate over Democratic nominee Abdul El-Sayed three days before Election Day after a weekend dispute over El-Sayed’s ties to Hasan and a super-PAC ad supporting El-Sayed that panelists linked to Hasan’s uncle, Cenk Uygur, and that blamed U.S. support for Israel for grocery and gas prices. Steve Hayes, Jonah Goldberg, Mike Warren, and Sarah Isgur reviewed Stevens’ narrow primary loss to El-Sayed, her vague post-primary support, her demand that he denounce the ad, and his refusal or partial condemnation; they also cited his reluctance to condemn the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps and his refusal to issue a statement on Ayatollah Ali Khamenei’s death as substantive concerns, while noting El-Sayed raised $1.2 million after the endorsement and a New York Times poll showed him leading Rogers 53-42. The panel also previewed the Alaska Senate race between Mary Peltola and Sen. Dan Sullivan, including a claimed Democratic effort to place another Dan Sullivan on the ballot to confuse voters and a Trump administration legal challenge, plus brief election-night predictions.

Actionable follow-up is political rather than health-related: monitor Michigan’s final polls and election-night margins to test whether Stevens’ endorsement moved voters in a race where she received about 49% of the Democratic primary vote, and compare El-Sayed’s $1.2 million fundraising surge against any late polling shift. Research El-Sayed’s full record on Israel, Hasan, the ad, the IRGC, and Khamenei, plus Stevens’ endorsement statement and Anderson Cooper interview, to assess whether her decision reflected principle, campaign slights, or both; also follow the Alaska duplicate-Dan Sullivan ballot litigation and the Trump administration challenge. Key questions are whether El-Sayed explicitly denounced the ad, whether pro-Israel Michigan Democrats changed their votes, whether the Alaska name-confusion tactic survived legal scrutiny, and whether the endorsement becomes a symbolic intra-party discipline moment rather than a measurable vote mover.
