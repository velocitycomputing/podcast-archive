## Summary

Sleep researcher Chris Barnes, interviewed by Shankar Vedantam on Hidden Brain, frames sleep deprivation as a systemic safety, judgment, and behavior problem rather than a simple personal failing, citing 50 to 70 million Americans with sleep disorders and almost 60% of adults in India getting less than six hours of sleep nightly. He recounts train engineer William Rockefeller’s December 2013 derailment after falling asleep on a route from Poughkeepsie to New York City, where the train reportedly reached about 80 mph in a 70 mph section and failed to slow for a 35 mph curve, killing 4 and injuring 60; the National Transportation Safety Board cited sleep apnea and a recent schedule change as contributing factors. Barnes also describes Gary Hart’s 2001 UK incident, in which falling asleep after driving through the night led to a vehicle on train tracks and a head-on train collision that killed 10 and injured 82, described as the worst UK rail disaster of the 21st century. Barnes’s own near-miss after a 3 a.m. hospital call, including 3 to 5 seconds of microsleep on I-5 at highway speeds, illustrates cumulative sleep debt. The episode also cites Barnes and Sam Yam’s Singapore and Taiwan data showing car-crash spikes after late-night European soccer matches, especially when popular teams play; Moscow taxi driver Chingis Anarbek Ulu’s 20-hour shift and mistaken accelerator press; research linking sleep loss to impaired non-routine decisions, unethical behavior, lower moral awareness, and irritability through weakened prefrontal cortex-amygdala regulation; BP’s 2005 Texas explosion, with 15 killed and 180 injured, after 12-hour shifts, long commutes, and a 43-hour sleep debt; nuclear disasters such as Chernobyl and Three Mile Island occurring between roughly 1:30 and 4 a.m.; and the March 11, 2018 New York City helicopter crash on daylight-saving Sunday, plus studies of spring daylight-saving crash and fatality spikes and mining-injury patterns.

Treat sleep loss as a safety and judgment risk: if you have choking awakenings, unrefreshing sleep despite time in bed, or daytime sleepiness, ask a clinician about sleep apnea and possible sleep evaluation; if you feel drowsy while driving, stop rather than push through; use short 10 to 15 minute naps when possible; protect sleep around schedule changes, night shifts, long commutes, and daylight-saving transitions; avoid making novel, high-stakes, ethical, or emotional decisions when sleep-deprived; and track sleep, errors, irritability, near-misses, and workplace incidents to identify patterns. Research leads include Barnes’s work on sleep, workplace ethics, moral awareness, and decision-making; Sam Yam’s soccer-crash studies; daylight-saving and mining-injury analyses; and circadian misalignment in safety-sensitive jobs. Follow-up questions should cover whether sleep disorders, shift timing, always-on habits, or accumulated sleep debt are driving impaired performance, risky choices, or mood problems.
