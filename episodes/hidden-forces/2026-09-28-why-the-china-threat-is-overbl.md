## Summary

Logan Wright, a China macro-finance analyst at Rhodium Group and author of *Broken China*, told Hidden Forces host Demetri Kofinas that China's economy is in structural decline and that its leadership has far less control over outcomes than the prevailing narrative assumes. Wright traced his path from 2001 fieldwork in China, when officials openly borrowed foreign models and reform looked genuinely convergent, to a break around 2011–12. He cited the Arab Spring revolutions and the Bo Xilai affair as triggers. He argues the later turn toward confrontation came from Beijing's policy choices, not from inevitability or Western containment, and he defends WTO entry as a rational bet. His central claim is that China had the largest credit boom and bust in a century, so the financial system that once drove growth and absorbed malinvestment now constrains it. Per his account, the post-2021 picture (Evergrande, persistent deflation, large fiscal deficits, difficulty writing down bad loans, rising reliance on exports) is what a country five years after a financial crisis looks like. He says headline GDP and industrial value added are the only series still showing stability, because the property collapse (housing starts down 40% in 2022, a sector worth 20–25% of GDP) is not reflected in them. He estimates true 2023 growth at roughly 1.5–2% against the reported 5.2%, and puts current growth below zero, with fixed-asset investment falling about 7% nominal. He says the inflated figures serve internal party signaling and a strategic message that China's rise is inevitable, aimed at discouraging de-risking, sanctions and export controls. He expects Beijing to eventually favor détente on trade. The transcript was truncated before the discussion of the post-2008 credit expansion and the Olympics and financial crisis.

For you, the practical takeaway is to treat official Chinese GDP and industrial output as unreliable. Cross-check them against independent indicators Wright named: Komatsu equipment hours worked, Alibaba's Taobao and Tmall sales, consumer and producer price trends, interest rates, capital flows, and property starts. Rhodium's China data work is the source he points to for tracking where money actually flows. If you hold China-exposed assets or make decisions that depend on Chinese demand, test the assumption that Beijing can stimulate its way back to 5% growth. His view implies deflationary export pressure and a policy shift toward trade accommodation, so those are scenarios to watch rather than counting on a US-China escalation path. Weigh the claims with care: his sub-zero growth estimate is his own and not independently verified here, and the real debate he concedes is over how much the figures are overstated, not whether they are. Reading *Broken China* directly would give you the full argument, and the unread truncated portion covers the 2008–2016 credit expansion in more detail.
