## Summary

Justin Mares, co-founder and CEO of TrueMed, spoke with Daisy Wolf about a narrow set of health interventions he considers high-return, including circadian rhythm, morning outdoor light, walking, protein quality, supplements, blood work, imaging, toxin reduction, and using HSA/FSA dollars for prevention. He described TrueMed as a platform that lets qualifying individuals spend tax-free HSA/FSA funds on lifestyle interventions such as gym memberships, Peloton, sleep aids, food, and supplements when accompanied by a doctor’s note for treating, reversing, or preventing disease, citing claims that about 90% of healthcare spending goes to chronic disease management while less than 3% goes to prevention and that the system may spend an average of $3 million managing a person’s type 2 diabetes after diagnosis. His routine includes 30–60 minutes outside soon after waking, coffee before 11 a.m., bone broth followed by fruit and honey until noon, workouts between 11 a.m. and 2 p.m., a treadmill desk for 4,000–5,000 extra steps, walking after meals and on internal calls, lunches of ground beef with organ meats, carrots, salad, and vegetables, and dinners of fish, vegetables, ground beef, or tacos, with emphasis on high sourcing standards. He argued that over-optimizing glucose spikes is misguided, that keto helped him initially but later affected his life, and that chicken may be worth limiting because monogastric animals fed corn and soy accumulate more polyunsaturated fatty acids, or PUFAs, in their tissue. He also discussed seed oils as highly processed, oxidized, and potentially linked to inflammation, fatty liver disease, and metabolic disease, noting that soybean oil now provides nearly 20% of average American calories versus almost 0% in 1970, while acknowledging this is a contested area. On protein, he said needs vary by sex, pregnancy, and activity level, estimated his own intake at 120–150 grams per day, and emphasized collagen, gelatin, and glycine as commonly missing nutrients. His supplements included a methylated B vitamin complex for the MTHFR mutation, which he said affects 30–40% of people; Maui Nui organ blend; a Thorn eye-support product for screen use; and Toku nattokinase, which he claimed may help prevent or break down arterial plaque and perform slightly better than statins, though that claim should be treated cautiously. For preventative care, he gets labs every six months, coronary calcium scans and Ezra full-body scans about every 18 months, and criticized the limited time average primary-care physicians spend with patients, saying many may not deeply understand markers like ApoB or Lp(a). He also named Brian Johnson as an example of extreme health optimization he does not follow, and discussed home toxin reduction through whole-house or reverse-osmosis water filtration, air filters, Branch Basics cleaning products, and avoiding Teflon and low-quality Tupperware.

Actionable takeaways include checking whether HSA/FSA dollars could be used for eligible prevention-related expenses with a doctor’s note, especially for gym access, sleep tools, food interventions, or supplements if the user is trying to treat, reverse, or prevent a specific condition. A practical next step is getting baseline labs, including cardiovascular markers such as ApoB, Lp(a), and LDL, plus any symptom-driven deficiencies, then testing one or two interventions for 90–180 days and retesting rather than stacking many changes at once. For daily habits, the highest-leverage moves suggested are 30–60 minutes of morning outdoor light, more walking after meals or during calls, a treadmill desk if feasible, regular strength training, and improving food quality by prioritizing whole, well-sourced foods while reducing highly processed seed oils and possibly limiting conventional chicken if PUFA intake is a concern. Protein should be individualized, but adding collagen, gelatin, bone broth, or high-quality protein powder may be useful for people with low intake, especially if they mainly eat muscle meats. For environment-related changes, installing a quality water filter, running air filters, switching to cleaner cleaning products, and replacing questionable cookware or food storage are concrete options. Supplement decisions should be tied to labs, symptoms, and clinician review: methylated B vitamins may be relevant if MTHFR or methylation issues are suspected; organ blends may be considered for nutrient density; eye-support supplements may be worth exploring for heavy screen use; and nattokinase or other cardiovascular supplements should be discussed with a clinician before use, especially if the user has heart risk factors, takes medications, or is considering alternatives to statins. Follow-up questions to consider are: What are my ApoB, Lp(a), LDL, inflammatory markers, and metabolic labs? Do I have family history of cardiovascular disease or cancer that would justify earlier or more frequent imaging? Which single habit—morning light, walking, protein/collagen, water filtration, air filtration, or seed-oil reduction—would be easiest to implement first? And what 90-day experiment can be measured with symptoms, performance, or repeat labs?
