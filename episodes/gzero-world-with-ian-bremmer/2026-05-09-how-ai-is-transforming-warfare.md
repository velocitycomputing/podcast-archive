## Summary

Katrina Manson, a Bloomberg defense reporter, discussed how the Pentagon's Project Maven—originally launched in 2017 to use machine learning for processing drone footage—has rapidly expanded into autonomous weapons, drone swarming, and AI-assisted targeting systems. The deployment pace outstrips reliability: algorithms fail when battlefield conditions shift, and the targeting workflow is accelerating to the point where operators mechanically approve AI recommendations with minimal scrutiny. Despite recognizing known vulnerabilities in military AI systems—including sycophancy, escalation, bias, and hallucination—the Pentagon has not yet implemented adequate safeguards.

For those monitoring military technology policy or working in AI governance, this episode underscores an urgent gap: the speed of AI deployment in weapons systems is outpacing the development of oversight mechanisms and technical solutions. The actionable takeaway is that current military AI systems lack meaningful human control over targeting decisions and have documented failure modes that remain unsolved. Stakeholders in defense policy, AI ethics, or national security should prioritize understanding and advocating for concrete safeguards—not theoretical frameworks—that would slow deployment to match the maturity of safety solutions.

Ian Bremmer's guest this week is author and Bloomberg defense tech reporter Katrina Manson, who spent years reporting on Project Maven for her new book on the Pentagon's AI push.
The program launched in 2017 with a narrow mandate: use machine learning to process drone footage. It has since expanded into something far more ambitious. Autonomous weapons, drone swarming technology, and AI-assisted targeting are now central to how the Pentagon talks about modern warfare.
The tech rollout is fast, but not reliable. Algorithms fail when the battlefield changes. The targeting process is accelerating to the point where operators are clicking through AI recommendations with little ability to question them. Manson says the military knows about AI's vulnerability "to sycophancy, to escalation, to bias and hallucination," and has not yet found adequate solutions.
