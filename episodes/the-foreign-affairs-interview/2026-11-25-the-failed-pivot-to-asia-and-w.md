## Summary

Mira Rapp-Hooper and Zack Cooper, with host Dan Kurtz Phalan, assessed Trump’s second-term China policy after his APEC trip to Shenzhen and meetings with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin, arguing Xi views the Trump rapprochement as a temporary window rather than a paradigm shift and has used U.S. concessions—reduced security assistance to Taiwan, scaled-back exercises, chip export-control relief—to increase pressure around Taiwan, the Senkaku Islands, the South China Sea, the Philippines, Laos, and Cambodia. They tied this to the Jensen Wong/NVIDIA export-control scandal, likely congressional action, the fall 2025 Busan truce on agriculture and critical minerals, and the optics of a Trump-Xi-Putin summit that gave Putin legitimacy and Xi a center-stage role while producing little substantive progress on structural U.S.-China issues. Cooper emphasized Asia’s lack of a European-style “plan B” if U.S. reliability erodes, warning allies may defect toward China; he judged 2027 relatively stable because of China’s party congress but 2028 riskier because of Taiwan and U.S. elections, while both he and Rapp-Hooper said PLA purges—leaving China’s Central Military Commission with two of seven members and reportedly purging much of the military leadership—make full-scale invasion unlikely but gray-zone coercion, quarantine, blockade, customs, law-enforcement, financial, economic, and cyber pressure more likely, with U.S. Coast Guard presence a weak spot.

No direct health implications are present; actionable follow-ups include tracking whether a Taiwan arms-sale package appears before the G20 or by late December/early January, whether Congress advances export-control or tech-competition measures after the Jensen Wong/NVIDIA disclosures, whether China responds with legal actions, exit bans, or retaliation against U.S. firms, and whether the Busan truce is extended or collapses over agriculture and critical-minerals commitments. Research leads include Rapp-Hooper’s piece with Eli Ratner on China exploiting the Trump window, Rapp-Hooper and Rebecca Listner’s “Spheres by Default,” Cooper’s writings on Taiwan and Asian balancing, and the “constructive strategic stability” framing from the summit. Decisions to consider include monitoring gray-zone indicators around Taiwan—coast guard/quarantine simulations, customs/administrative coercion, maritime blockades, cyber/financial pressure—rather than only invasion readiness, asking whether U.S. Coast Guard, economic, financial, and cyber deterrence signals are sufficient to reassure allies and deter China without triggering conflict, and noting that both Washington and Beijing may believe time is on their side, a confidence gap that could make miscalculation more likely.
