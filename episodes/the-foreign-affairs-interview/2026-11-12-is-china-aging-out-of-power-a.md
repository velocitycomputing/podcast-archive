## Summary

In the Foreign Affairs Interview, host Dan Kurtz Phalan and Carl Minzner, a China scholar at Fordham University and the Council on Foreign Relations, discuss China’s demographic decline and its intersection with political paralysis under Xi Jinping. They note that China’s population peaked at 1.4 billion in 2021 and is now falling by roughly 3 to 4 million people per year, while the median age has risen from about 23 in 1990 to around 41 today and is projected to reach about 50 by mid-century; the share of people aged 65 and older is about 15% now, projected to reach 30% by mid-century and 40% to 50% late in the century. Minzner compares China with Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong, and Macau, citing fertility rates of about 0.8 in South Korea, 0.7 in Taiwan, 1.1 in Japan, 1.68 in the United States, and around 1 in China, along with marriage rates that fell by about 50% in China and South Korea between the early 2010s and early 2020s, very low rates of births outside marriage in East Asia, and rising single-person households. He argues that China’s coercive one-child policy, the 2016 relaxation, the 2020 census shock, and the 2021 shift toward parental leave, daycare, and baby bonuses have not reversed the trend, while Xi’s personalist rule, an all-male Politburo, regressive gender messaging, and failure to adopt migration and elder-care cushions make a mid-21st-century China more frail, sclerotic, and brittle; he also warns that the United States remains dependent on immigration, which with births from immigration accounted for about 86% of U.S. population growth.

For practical use, treat this as a demographic and geopolitical risk brief: track China’s fertility rate, marriage rate, births outside marriage, median age, 65-and-older share, working-age population, retirement-age policy, elder-care spending, pension solvency, migration inflows, and official family-planning language, while benchmarking against Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong, and Macau. If health implications are relevant, prioritize long-term care, chronic disease, dementia, mental health, social isolation, caregiver burden, and labor shortages in health and social services, because a rapidly aging and more atomized society will increase demand for elder support and strain public finances. Useful research leads include Minzner’s Foreign Affairs essay “China’s Long Twilight,” East Asian fertility and marriage data, studies on conservative gender norms and women’s labor participation, migration as a demographic cushion, and U.S. immigration’s role in population growth. Decisions and follow-up questions should focus on whether China will expand migrant labor, raise retirement ages, fund elder care, reduce marriage and childbearing penalties for women, or instead rely on coercion and moral suasion; also ask whether U.S. policy is prepared for slower growth if immigration declines and whether institutions are ready for aging-related health and workforce pressures.
