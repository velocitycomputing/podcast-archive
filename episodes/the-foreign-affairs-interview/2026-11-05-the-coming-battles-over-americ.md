## Summary

Host Dan Kurtz Phalan framed the November 4 midterms as a supercharged start to fights over Trump’s remaining foreign policy and the post-Trump future of both parties, with Kori Schake—AEI director of foreign and defense policy studies and a former State Department and NSC official under George W. Bush—and Thomas Wright—Brookings senior fellow and former Biden NSC senior director for strategic planning—arguing that Democratic House and Senate victories will intensify congressional-executive and intra-party battles. They discussed Vice President J.D. Vance’s claim that the Iran war caused Republican losses; Schake said tariffs and broader administration policies caused affordability pain, while Wright saw Vance’s position as political positioning after earlier leaks. Wright said Congress can constrain Trump mainly through confirmation fights over senior officials such as a defense secretary or Rubio replacement, appropriations, hearings, reports on Russia’s shadow war or Ukraine campaign, and enforcement of export controls under the MACCH Act and the protect agenda, where he said near veto-proof majorities exist and Commerce has been gutted against the Davis Sachs/Jensen Wong approach. He also saw Ukraine as a possible margin-of-change area via unspent appropriated funds, litigation, and new aid, while Schake warned Democrats not to grill recently retired officers fired by the Pentagon because civil-military norms require accepting presidential firing authority and military compliance with disliked policies. The conversation covered Trump’s possible Iran choices—escalation, declaring victory, or a low-grade war—possible North Korea recognition or Latin America focus, possible troop withdrawals, and Wright’s probability estimates that the Ukraine war has a 50% chance of continuing into 2029, a 10–15% chance of Russian-imposed terms, and a 35–40% chance of ending next spring or summer. They also debated whether allies such as Europe, South Korea, Japan, Canada, and middle powers are permanently hedging against American power, with Schake suggesting many will hope for a post-Trump reversion to reliability and Wright warning that 2029 may be the most difficult strategic environment since the Cold War because of wars, erratic leaders, nationalism, and damaged U.S. institutions.

No health implications are present; the actionable items are policy-monitoring and research leads: track whether Trump settles or prolongs the Iran war, whether a freedom-of-navigation coalition for the Strait of Hormuz and Babo-Mendeb emerges, and whether Congress uses appropriations, lawsuits, or troop-stationing floors to force Ukraine support or limit Caribbean/Latin America military actions. Follow-up questions should ask whether OMB director Russ Vought remains, whether defense and State leadership changes alter policy, whether Commerce can actually enforce export controls on high-end chips and manufacturing equipment, and whether Congress can convert Ukraine funding into spent aid despite presidential refusal. Research leads include civil-military relations after officer purges, the legal and political limits of presidential foreign-policy discretion, the durability of tariffs as an affordability issue, the Davis Sachs/Jensen Wong export-control debate, middle-power hedging and European strategic autonomy, and scenario planning for a Ukraine ceasefire or frozen-line deal by 2029. Decisions to consider include prioritizing oversight of implementation and personnel over symbolic hearings, watching state and congressional actions such as New Jersey’s Russian-diesel warning and NDAA troop floors, and preparing for a 2029 environment in which U.S. institutional capacity may need rebuilding before it can be used abroad.
