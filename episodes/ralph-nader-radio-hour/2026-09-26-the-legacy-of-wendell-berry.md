## Summary

The episode memorializes Wendell Berry, the recently deceased novelist, poet, and farmer whose life work critiqued industrial agriculture and celebrated the relationship between people and land. His daughter Mary Berry, executive director of the Berry Center in Newcastle, Kentucky (founded 2011), discusses her father's singular legacy: his refusal to separate talk of land from talk of people. The segment explores Berry's devastating critique of global free trade—which ignores real costs like loss of local production capacity, cultural disruption, economic justice, and ecological damage—and covers the Berry family's multigenerational agricultural activism dating to 1908, when Mary's grandfather created protective legislation that stabilized farm economics in Kentucky for 60+ years. Mary describes her own farming evolution from dairy and tobacco to organic beef, vegetables, and the first CSA in Kentucky, and explains the crisis facing rural Kentucky since the tobacco program's end: the loss of 15,000 farms and the rural communities that depended on them.

For action, visit theberrycenter.org to learn about their four programs, particularly "Our Home Place Meet" (working with young farmers on protective economic principles) and the Farm and Forest Institute (teaching practical farming skills on their 200-acre farm). The center fills a gap: it serves small-to-mid-scale farmers cut off from information sources dominated by land-grant universities, the Farm Bureau, and industrial agriculture corporations. If you care about food security and rural viability, support organizations that help farmers access land, capital, market information, and cultural knowledge outside corporate channels—the Berry Center explicitly focuses on "farming in the middle," not hobby gardening, and welcomes visitors and engagement.
