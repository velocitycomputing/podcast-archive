## Summary

Sebastian Junger, author and war correspondent, discusses his near-death experience from a 2020 ruptured aneurysm and its profound aftermath on EconTalk. While clinically dying, he witnessed a vision of his deceased father—a striking phenomenon despite being a lifelong atheist. This experience prompted Junger to investigate near-death experiences, the physics of consciousness, and why dying people commonly report encounters with the dead. He reflects on decades covering wars from Sarajevo to Afghanistan, connecting those experiences of mortality and trauma to insights gained from his own brush with death, exploring how confronting life's fragility reshapes perspective on meaning and existence.

For the listener, Junger's account offers a concrete framework for reconsidering mortality not as a morbid topic but as a clarifying force. His willingness to examine near-death phenomena scientifically—rather than dismissing them—suggests actionable value in honestly examining your own fears about death and what they reveal about what you actually value. His decades of war reporting demonstrate that sustained exposure to human fragility, whether through studying it or surviving it, can fundamentally shift what feels urgent or sacred in daily life. The episode invites you to ask: what would confronting your own mortality clarify about your current priorities and relationships?

## Transcript

What does a lifelong atheist do when his dead father appears above him in the emergency room? Author and war reporter Sebastian Junger nearly bled to death in 2020 from a ruptured aneurysm, and what he saw in those moments sent him on a journey into physics, near-death experiences, and the nature of consciousness itself. In his third appearance on EconTalk, Junger discusses his remarkable book In My Time of Dying with host Russ Roberts. He reflects on covering wars from Sarajevo to Afghanistan, the strange phenomenon of dying people seeing the dead, and why he's still an atheist. Along the way, Junger offers a powerful meditation on terror and reverence, blessing and wounding, and why understanding life's fragility might be the most sacred gift of all.
