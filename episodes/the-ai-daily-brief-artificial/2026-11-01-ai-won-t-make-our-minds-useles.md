## Summary

Venkatesh Rao’s essay “cognitive mercantilism,” discussed on The AI Daily Brief, argues that AI panic mirrors 17th-century mercantilism: intelligence is treated as scarce bullion to be hoarded by elites, ordinary people are distrusted, supply-side control of brains/IP/AI dominates, and most people are assumed to be intellectually useless if automated. Rao uses Joyce Appleby’s The Relentless Revolution and 400 years of economic history to compare capitalism’s emergence to AI’s transition: early modern Britain escaped zero-sum thinking when wealth was seen as creatable, agricultural productivity freed most people from farming, and new consumer desires for tobacco, tea, sugar, and Indian cottons raised voluntary work from about 250 to 300 days per year. The host connects this to demand elasticity—price, access, complexity, continuity, personalization, and relational—and to the “infinite backlog” idea, while sponsor segments mention Robots and Pencils, CData, Granola, and Blitzcy. The episode also contrasts AI Malthusian fears that intelligence will outrun human absorption with a cognitive-capitalist view of almost limitless demand, claiming AI may create a “cognitive industrious revolution” in which abundant intelligence expands demand, makes previously impossible projects feasible, and shifts politics from preserving cognitive jobs to securing fair access to intelligence, rather than producing “useless minds.”

Actionable takeaways: treat AI adoption as a demand-side experiment, not just automation; map tasks where AI lowers price, access, complexity, continuity, personalization, or relational barriers, then test whether those changes unlock new projects, customers, or learning rather than only cutting labor. For personal work, decide which parts of the “infinite backlog” to automate and which to keep as human judgment, and set explicit stopping rules to avoid the transcript’s “can’t sleep because I want to build one more thing” pattern; monitor sleep, stress, and compulsive productivity as health risks. Research leads include Appleby’s The Relentless Revolution, Rao’s “cognitive mercantilism,” demand-elasticity frameworks, and the analogy between agricultural labor liberation and cognitive labor liberation. Follow-up questions: Which intelligence products would ordinary people consume if they could command them? Which institutions gate access to AI? How should access, training, IP, open weights, and safety be governed so abundance does not become elite hoarding? What metrics show cognitive abundance—new projects, learning, creative output, well-being—rather than only output volume?
