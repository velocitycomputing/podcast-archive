## Summary

Stacey Schiff, author of The Witches: Salem, 1692, frames witchcraft as a pan-cultural concept, citing ancient scriptures, the first documented witch trial in ancient Greece, and witch hunts across Europe—especially Germany and France—and in Switzerland, Russia, Turkey, Sweden, carried out by Protestants, Catholics, Jews, Muslims, and animists; she links witch-trial frenzies to political dislocation, fear, and blame. The chapter then ties that pattern to J.K. Rowling’s 1990s: her mother died at 45 from MS, she suffered a miscarriage, left an abusive marriage, and fell into depression before Harry Potter’s record-breaking success. It situates the backlash in 1990s tensions—economic growth, internet optimism, gay-rights advances and backlash (Ellen DeGeneres, Colorado Amendment 2, parental advisories), LA riots (63 deaths), Clinton impeachment, Ritalin/Prozac debates, 24-hour news, Waco (76 deaths, including 25 children), Oklahoma City (168 deaths, including 19 children), and Columbine (35 shot, 14 killed)—while noting false media narratives about goth teens, Marilyn Manson, bullying, and antidepressants, plus unfounded “She Said Yes” martyr stories and Jared Stacey’s account of evangelical persecution fears. It details Christian opposition from John Hagee, James Dobson/Focus on the Family, school-board fights in Virginia, Texas, New Mexico, California, and Arkansas, the Cedarville, Arkansas case involving lawyer David Hogue, Matt Latimer’s claim that Bush administration figures opposed Rowling’s Presidential Medal of Freedom over witchcraft concerns, and broader fears about Wicca and pop-culture witchcraft (The Craft, Sabrina, Buffy, Charmed, Practical Magic, The Witches of Eastwick), with Rowling stating she does not believe in magic.

For the user, the actionable material is a research and decision checklist: treat claims that fantasy books cause harm as evidence questions, not just culture-war claims, by checking primary sources such as school-board records, the Cedarville, Arkansas litigation, Matt Latimer’s memoir, and debunked Columbine narratives like She Said Yes; compare the 1990s moral-panic template—political insecurity, 24-hour news, fear of youth subcultures, and medication debates around Ritalin/Prozac—with today’s book-ban and social-media controversies; and, if the user is making parenting, education, or mental-health choices, separate documented risks (abuse, depression, miscarriage, suicide/violence exposure) from speculative harms (witchcraft, goth fashion, fictional magic), seek evidence-based mental-health support rather than moral framing, and ask follow-up questions such as what empirical evidence links Harry Potter to harm, how school libraries should balance parental objection with access, what safeguards are appropriate for children’s mental-health treatment, and whether current outrage is driven by verified danger or by the same archetypal witch-hunt dynamics Schiff describes.
