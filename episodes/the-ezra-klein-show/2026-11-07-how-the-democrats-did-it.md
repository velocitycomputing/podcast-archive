## Summary

Michelle Goldberg and Ezra Klein examined how Democrats won unexpected races in Texas and Georgia despite a Trump-driven realignment, arguing the wins came from a combination of Trump’s unpopularity, strong candidates, full-ticket organizing, and localized populist messaging. They highlighted James Talarico’s Senate campaign in Texas—his “love your neighbor” religious framing, attacks on tech billionaires and data centers, and appeal to voters exhausted by rage—alongside Gina Hinojosa’s gubernatorial campaign against Greg Abbott, which tied data centers, water, school vouchers, and defunded public schools to elite capture. They noted Democrats won four of five redistricted House seats, the governorship, and large Texas House gains, with heavily Hispanic border counties swinging sharply toward Democrats versus 2024: Cameron County +44, Hidalgo County +49, and Webb County +50. They also discussed John Ossoff beating Mike Collins by 18 points in Georgia by linking Trumpism, January 6, corruption, and local costs such as health insurance and hospital closures, while invoking Joe Rogan’s early boost of Talarico, Trump’s role in selecting Ken Paxton, Elon Musk and Sam Altman as symbols of tech-bro resentment, the 2018 Beto O’Rourke comparison, and parallels to Peter Magyar in Hungary.

For practical follow-up, treat the episode as a checklist for assessing political and health-related local power: ask candidates and officials what they will do about data-center contracts, water and electricity impacts, noise, property-value effects, school-voucher cuts, after-school busing, health-insurance premiums, hospital closures, and immigration-enforcement stress in communities. Research leads include the Texas border-county swing, Hispanic voter movement, the “ticket effect” of competitive down-ballot races, religious-left moral framing, anti-billionaire populism, and corruption messaging that ties elite abuse to household costs. Decisions to consider are whether to support candidates who combine local material issues with non-rage messaging, whether to attend or fund down-ballot races rather than only marquee races, and whether to press elected officials for transparency on data-center tax breaks, utility and water agreements, school funding, and health-care access. Follow-up questions should include: Which local data centers or AI infrastructure projects have environmental or health impacts? Which school budgets were affected by vouchers? Which hospitals or insurance markets changed? What enforcement actions have caused fear or disrupted families? How are candidates proposing to lower costs and protect public resources?
